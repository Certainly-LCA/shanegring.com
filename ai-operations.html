AI Operations

AI operations: what to automate, what to leave alone, and why most pilots stall.

Every small business has now been sold an AI tool. Most of those tools are sitting unused, because they were bought before anyone wrote down how the work actually gets decided. I'm an AI consultant for small business who starts at the other end: get the operating logic out of your head and onto paper first, and the automation becomes obvious, safe, and cheap. Here's what AI operations means, where it pays first, and what it costs.

What is AI operations?

AI operations is running the repeatable work of a business with software and AI instead of people, by first writing down the decision rules that work follows. The written rules are the whole trick. They're what the automation is held to, what makes it safe to let go of, and what lets you tell whether it's doing the job.

Note the order. Most "AI consulting" starts with a tool and looks for somewhere to put it. That's backwards, and it's why the tools end up unused. The work that pays starts with the question no software can answer for you: how does this business actually decide things?

Why most small business AI pilots stall

The pattern is consistent enough to be boring. A tool gets bought. Someone points it at a task. The output is roughly right, nobody can say how right, and there's no standard to check it against — because the standard only ever existed in one person's head. So every result gets reviewed by the founder, which costs more than doing the work did, and within a month the tool is quietly abandoned.

The model almost never fails. The definition does. You cannot hand work to software for the same reason you couldn't hand it to the last person you hired: the task was never the hard part. The hundred small judgment calls around the task were, and those are still undocumented.

Which means the businesses that get real leverage out of AI aren't the ones with the best tools. They're the ones whose operating logic was already written down — and that gap is widening every quarter. The long version of this argument is in everything still runs through you.

Where automation actually pays first

A useful filter: automate work that is high-volume, low-judgment, and already has a right answer. In most businesses under $10M that means four places.

  • Moving data between tools. The quiet tax. Someone re-keys the same record into the CRM, the invoice, the project tool and the spreadsheet. There is no judgment here at all, and it's usually the fastest thing to remove.
  • Chasing status. Asking where something is, then telling someone else. A large share of most operations roles is this, and it disappears entirely once the systems report on themselves.
  • First drafts of repeated documents. Scopes, proposals, reports, follow-ups. The rule is that AI writes the first version against your standard and a person approves it — never that AI sends anything.
  • Routing and triage. Deciding which inbound thing goes where and how urgent it is. Automatable precisely because you can write the rule down — and worth doing early, because it's the decision that most often routes to the founder by default.

And the other half of the job, which matters more: what to leave alone. Anything where being wrong is expensive, anything where the rule isn't written yet, and anything that is actually your judgment rather than a process — pricing exceptions, the difficult client conversation, which bets to make. Automating an undefined decision doesn't speed it up. It just makes the error arrive faster and with more confidence.

What this looks like in practice

DRVN is the clearest example I have, because it's the same move twice. The first job there was taking a golf coaching methodology out of the founder's head and turning it into a certification other coaches could run. The second was doing that to how the business watches itself.

DRVN had the usual four windows onto performance: Search Console, Google Analytics, their ad traffic, and Shopify. Four dashboards, four different stories, and nobody with a free afternoon to reconcile them. They weren't short of data. They were short of a reading of it. So we built a custom agent that pulls all four together and answers what the separate dashboards can't: how people actually move through the site, which search terms lead to which pieces of content, and where impressions turn into sales rather than just traffic. Deliberately a small first step — it watched, and changed nothing.

Then it began making the corrections it could see were needed, week by week. And that's where the real work turned out to be, and why this page argues what it argues: the hard part was never building the agent. It was deciding what it's allowed to change on its own and what it has to flag for a person. Every one of those calls is a written rule that didn't exist before — and writing them down is exactly what made the automation safe to trust.

Across the first eight weeks of running it, weekly search clicks were up around 45%. The number I'd actually point at, though, is the one underneath: DRVN's average position didn't improve over that stretch — it drifted slightly the wrong way — while click-through rate rose about 15%. The rankings didn't get better. The same rankings started getting chosen more often. That's the part a weekly correction loop can move, and it's why I'd rather show you that number than a growth chart.

That list of rules keeps growing, and each addition hands back a bit more of the week. Where it's heading is a site that answers its own performance feedback: it notices what's working, adjusts for it, and keeps competing for the terms and the buyers DRVN actually wants — without waiting for a person to find the time to look.

Three ways in, and how to tell which you need

"AI consultant" covers three quite different purchases. I do all three — but they're not interchangeable, and buying the wrong one is how the money gets wasted. What decides it is whether your decision rules are already written down.

Extract first, then automate

When the process lives in someone's head. Slower to a first demo, because the first job is getting the rules onto paper. It's the default for a reason: most businesses at this size think they have a process and actually have a person. This is where the Operating Map goes.

Just build the thing

When you already know exactly what the rule is. Faster and cheaper, and genuinely the right call when the process is settled and written. I'll happily start here. The only trap is being sure — automating a process nobody has actually agreed on just produces a fast wrong answer.

Work out the plan first

When the question is where to start at all. Sequencing, what's worth doing, what to leave. Useful when there are several candidates and no obvious first one. Below about $10M I'd keep this short — there's rarely anyone to hand a roadmap to, so it should end in work, not a deck.

If you're weighing me against someone else, the question worth asking either of us is the same: what do I have after you leave? If the answer is a pilot and a login, you've bought a dependency. It should be written rules and systems your team can run and change without the person who built them.

AI operations FAQ

  • Do I need to have picked tools first? No, and it's better if you haven't. The tool choice is close to the last decision, not the first, and it changes depending on what the rules turn out to be.
  • Will this replace my team? That's not the aim and it's rarely the outcome. What it removes is the data-shuffling and status-chasing your people do instead of the work you hired them for.
  • We already tried an AI pilot and it went nowhere. That's the common starting point, and it's usually diagnostic rather than damning — it means the process wasn't written down. The pilot was fine; it had nothing to be accountable to.
